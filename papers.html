Peter Brandt (Factor LLC), August 2022

The Dreaded "D" Word: A Discussion of Drawdowns

An August 2022 essay by a veteran trader on why drawdowns are inevitable and how to survive them. It defines drawdowns by depth and duration, measured on a NAV/VAMI basis, and argues most traders live either in a drawdown or recovering from one — even Bitcoin spent 36 months underwater. It separates drawdowns that end in destruction (no plan, weak conviction, holy-grail hopping, oversized bets, bailing at lows, edgeless systems) from drawdowns within long-term profitability (plan out of sync with markets, trader out of sync with plan, ill-timed experimentation). A section on randomized outcomes shows that identical trade metrics, shuffled through a randomizer, produce wildly different equity curves — losing streaks can be pure statistical probability. It closes with a prescription: build a personalized process, then take the leap of faith with small size, discipline, and a monetary stop-loss.

  • Drawdowns are inevitable for every career trader; handling adversity matters more than handling winning periods.
  • Track drawdowns by depth and duration on a NAV/VAMI basis; obsessing over daily account value breeds irrational decisions.
  • Most traders spend most of their careers either in a drawdown or recovering from one.
  • Account-killers: no trading plan, lack of conviction, holy-grail hopping, 5-10%+ bet sizes, and quitting at drawdown lows.
  • Resist overhauling a historically profitable method during a drawdown; the author's 2013-14 experimentation made his drawdown two-to-three times longer.
  • Identical trade metrics run through a randomizer produce wildly different equity curves — brutal streaks can be pure chance.
  • Commit fully for three years: start small, execute with discipline, and set a monetary stop-loss around 30%.
  • The hardest part is the leap of faith — not knowing if a drawdown is normal variance or a broken edge.

Amos Hostetter: A Successful Speculator's Approach to Commodities Trading

The legendary internal Commodities Corporation booklet, written after Amos Hostetter's death in January 1977 to preserve the trading philosophy of the firm's mentor. His approach: absorb every fundamental fact ("broad brush" supply/demand analysis) but follow fundamentals only if and as long as the charts do not cast a negative vote. Money management came first — keep losses small, size by risk/reward, rarely risk even 10% of equity, and voluntarily scale down after setbacks. Technicals ranked trend following, support/resistance, and pattern recognition, backed by concrete rules: the 60% retracement safety net, the six-day and thirty-day rules, false-breakout signals, and reading price response to news. Appendices include his 1966 pre-trade questionnaire and verbatim maxims on the dangers human nature poses to traders.

  • "Take care of your losses and the profits will take care of themselves" — his core money-management principle.
  • Follow the fundamentals in your trading, but only if and as long as the charts do not cast a negative vote.
  • Never underestimate the power of the major trend; big money is made in big moves — patience over hastiness.
  • Rarely risk even 10% of equity on one idea; after setbacks, voluntarily scale down to token size.
  • Safety nets: exit longs if a reaction retraces 60% of the prior upmove; sell on a break of the preceding six days' low.
  • Surprising price response to news is a top forecaster: when a market doesn't do what it "should," it soon does what it "shouldn't."
  • Pre-trade questionnaire: major trend confirmed, trade with it, 10%+ move expected, stop chosen, reward at least twice risk — one "no" kills the trade.
  • Human-nature dangers: fear, hope, laziness, and believing what pleases you instead of sticking to facts.

Commodities Corporation: The Michael Marcus Tape

Annotated notes on the archived Michael Marcus training video from Commodities Corporation, written by a veteran trader who worked alongside Comm. Corp traders at Cocoa Merchants Ltd. He describes Comm. Corp as a 'trading university' that archived every trader's philosophy and required 'knocked out-of-the-box' post-mortems after losses. Core Marcus lessons: take a 'sacred position' only when fundamentals, charts and market action all agree; understand the trade-off between stop distance and loss size; develop 'market feel' by converting news into expected price change; risk a fixed percent of equity per trade; and enter as close to the 'danger point' (stop-out) as possible to protect both financial and mental capital. The author adds stories on Optimal F, Richard Dennis's Turtles, gut feel, and finishing second to Paul Tudor Jones in Comm. Corp's $35,000 Trader Evaluation Program.

  • The 'sacred position': load up only when fundamentals, charts and market action all agree; then tight stops rarely get hit.
  • Trading runs on two capitals, financial and mental; losing either knocks you out of business, so protect both equally.
  • Your edge or system must match your personality; a disconnect between method and temperament makes success impossible.
  • Enter as close to the 'danger point' (stop-out level) as possible: small survivable losses, asymmetric payoff, restful sleep.
  • Be willing to spend paper profits rather than real equity; it is the only way to ride winners.
  • Only about six markets get hot in a normal year; trade like a floor trader until ahead, then trend-follow.
  • Market feel means converting news into expected price change; Marcus said without feel you can never be a great trader.
  • Optimal F maximizes long-run returns but its equity swings are psychologically untradeable for almost everyone.

Peter Brandt, The Factor Report (November 3, 2022)

Which Performance Metric Really Predicts Trading Success (Twitter Poll Commentary)

Peter Brandt's Factor Report commentary on his November 2022 Twitter poll asking which metric best correlates with long-term trading success: win rate, Gain-to-Pain ratio, Calmar ratio, or rate-of-return. The correct answer, Gain-to-Pain ratio (GPR), won the vote; Brandt notes 46% of voters 'wasted their vote' on win rate and raw return. Win rate is meaningless without the ratio of average win to average loss, and rate-of-return ignores the volatility and drawdowns endured to achieve it. He defines and grades GPR (his Schwager-calculated career GPR is 2.8), Calmar, and his favorite metric, Profit Factor (his prop account's PF since 2014 is 5.4), while dismissing the Sharpe Ratio for penalizing the upside volatility great trading requires. Serious traders must track performance metrics like a business tracks margins.

  • Win rate is a nearly meaningless metric; profitable traders exist at 30% win rates and blow-ups at 70%.
  • What matters is average win size relative to average loss size, not how often you are right.
  • Raw rate-of-return is meaningless without the drawdowns endured; a 50% return with 50% drawdowns is no achievement.
  • Gain-to-Pain ratio (sum of monthly gains over sum of losses): 1.0 acceptable, 2.0 outstanding, 4.0 world class.
  • Sharpe Ratio is mostly useless for traders: it penalizes upside volatility, exactly the volatility outstanding profitability needs.
  • Profit Factor, measured per trade rather than per month, reveals more with fewer data; above 5.0 is world class.
  • Trading is a business; serious traders must maintain and study performance metrics to find strengths and weaknesses.

Milton W. Berg, CFA — MB Advisors, LLC (Report No. 672, July 5, 2015)

We Never Deal in Certainties: Approach to the Markets

Milton Berg outlines his market approach, starting with Benjamin Graham's own admission that value is only one factor in market price — the market is a voting machine driven partly by emotion, and Graham could never rationally explain why price converges to value. Berg shows Graham's history-based indicators and his 1946 'Dow never breaks out permanently' call failed, arguing markets are economic, not physical, systems: unique unrepeatable factors make conventional statistical analysis invalid for prices. Yet extremes are informative. Tracking 30,000+ indicators, Berg demonstrates with two public examples — 5-day S&P 500 rate-of-change moves of ±11% and extreme TRIN readings (15.50 in 1988, 12.50 in 2011) — that statistical extremes cluster at major turning points. The discipline: most days say nothing; wait for the extreme, then act on the probabilities.

  • Even Graham conceded value is one factor among many; the market is a voting machine, part reason, part emotion.
  • Graham couldn't explain why price converges to value: 'That is one of the mysteries of our business.'
  • Markets are economic systems, not physical ones — conventional statistical analysis doesn't validly predict prices.
  • Graham's 1946 call that the Dow never breaks out permanently failed; past ranges don't bind the future.
  • Most daily market action is random noise; indicators say nothing of value on most days.
  • Statistical extremes (±11% 5-day moves, extreme TRIN) cluster near major turning points.
  • The key discipline: wait for the statistical extreme, then act on it.
  • Effective indicators must signal non-randomly — at lows, highs, or accelerations — else ignore them.

Milton W. Berg, CFA (Duquesne Capital Management) — Journal of Technical Analysis, Issue 65, 2008 (MTA)

The Boundaries of Technical Analysis

Berg's MTA journal paper challenges academics (Roberts, Ibbotson, Sinquefield, Siegel) who treat stock prices as random dice rolls with a fixed positive bias. Markets are economic, not physical, systems: historical returns need not be replicable, so conventional statistics misapply to prices. Yet randomness has an exploitable exception — Stephen Jay Gould's drunkard's-walk 'reflecting boundary': a random walker beside a wall drifts predictably away from it. Berg posits such reflecting boundaries exist in markets (low valuation being one contributor) and shows how to detect them: 5-day rate-of-change extremes (±8%) occurring within days of 90-day or six-month lows, 250-day volume spikes, and extreme TRIN readings. Combined, these price-time-volume signals flagged nearly every historic S&P 500 bottom (1929, 1932, 1962, 1974, 1982, 2002) and major tops, redefining technical analysis as boundary detection.

  • Markets aren't loaded dice; economic systems mutate, so historical return statistics may never be replicable.
  • Gould's drunkard's walk: random motion beside a 'reflecting boundary' still produces predictable directional drift.
  • Value investing works because low valuation is a technical indicator of a reflecting boundary, not a law.
  • The only certain information in prices is what buyers and sellers agreed to pay.
  • Study tails of return distributions: extreme 5-day moves near 90-day lows mark major bottoms.
  • Combining price, time, and volume extremes (ROC, 250-day volume, TRIN) pinpoints capitulation.
  • TRIN below 0.50 plus volume spike: bullish at 1-year lows, bearish at 3-year highs.
  • Technical indicators reveal symptoms of turning points, not causes of market movement.

Andy Constan, The Damped Spring Report (Damped Spring Advisors)

The Script: The Only Way to Kill Inflation (Damped Spring Report, July 2023)

A July 2023 macro report laying out a five-act 'script' for how the Fed can finally kill inflation. Act 1 is 'Higherer for Longerer Island': hikes continue but fail because long-end yields stay pinned below 4%, asset prices stay bid, and companies won't fire workers while their stocks are strong. The play only advances when a supply catalyst — Treasury shifting from bills to coupon issuance, faster QT or outright SOMA sales, bank deleveraging, or China/Japan FX-defense bond selling — pushes long yields and term premium up (Act 2), compressing equity multiples (Act 3), contracting earnings and demand (Act 4), and finally producing recession and job losses (Act 5). Includes trade implications per act and the firm's risk-premium framework built on money/credit availability and expected portfolio risk.

  • Rate hikes alone won't kill inflation: companies won't fire workers while their share prices are strong.
  • Long-end yields stay low without bond supply; term premium was deeply negative, meaning bonds offered negative expected return vs cash.
  • The catalyst must be policy: Treasury coupon issuance, faster QT or Fed sales, bank deleveraging, or China/Japan currency defense.
  • Sequence matters: bond supply -> higher yields -> multiple compression -> earnings contraction -> recession. Each act requires the prior one.
  • Risk premium has two drivers: money/credit available for asset purchases vs supply, and expected portfolio risk (vol plus diversification).
  • Low realized vol plus high diversification benefit invites leverage; when both reverse, risk premium expands and levered portfolios unwind.
  • Wealth effects run the demand cycle: falling asset prices precede job losses, and payroll drawdowns lag earnings drawdowns.
  • Have a script with explicit triggers per stage, and trade each stage differently rather than betting on chop.

Dr. Rhonda Patrick, FoundMyFitness

The Cognitive Enhancement Blueprint: Protocols for Reducing Brain Aging (BDNF Guide)

A practical guide to raising brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), the molecule driving neurogenesis and neuroplasticity, which declines with age and correlates with learning, memory, mood, and disease risk. Vigorous exercise is the lead intervention: lactate produced by intense effort crosses the blood-brain barrier and triggers BDNF, with HIIT outperforming easy steady-state work. Heat exposure (sauna, hot baths), omega-3s, flavonoid-rich foods and cocoa, zinc, meditation, mental training, probiotics, caloric restriction, and low-carb diets all raise BDNF as well, while the typical Western diet lowers it. The guide includes 'Rhonda's Protocols' — concrete weekly routines (outdoor runs at 80-85% max HR, HIIT cycling, Norwegian 4x4, dry sauna 3-4x weekly, EPA/DHA dosing) — and notes BDNF's wider role as a biomarker for depression, Parkinson's, and stroke recovery.

  • BDNF drives neurogenesis and neuroplasticity — the biological basis of learning and memory — and declines with age.
  • Intensity matters: lactate from vigorous exercise crosses into the brain and triggers BDNF; HIIT beats easy steady-state.
  • About 40 minutes at 65%+ max heart rate raised BDNF roughly one-third; even 30 minutes improves memory for 24 hours.
  • Six minutes of all-out cycling intervals raised circulating BDNF four to five times more than 90 easy minutes.
  • Heat works too: a 20-minute 42C bath raised BDNF two-thirds; regular sauna beat light exercise over ten weeks.
  • Diet levers: omega-3s (2g+ daily), high-flavonoid foods and cocoa, zinc, and probiotics all raise BDNF; Western diets lower it.
  • Meditation and novel mental training raise BDNF; three-month retreat meditators tripled plasma BDNF and cut anxiety scores 60 percent.
  • Caloric restriction of 25 percent raised BDNF by 50 percent; adding HIIT to a low-carb diet added 38 percent.

Paul Tudor Jones / Tudor Investment Corporation (faxed copy via Warburg Dillon Read, 2000)

Tudor Risk Control Manual & Paul Tudor Jones Risk Control Memo

A scanned copy of Tudor Investment Corporation's internal Risk Control Manual, opening with Paul Tudor Jones's famous December 1994 memo to all traders ('risk 2.5% to make 30%') describing escalating time-outs, trade freezes and written post-mortems at each drawdown threshold, plus his rules for pressing profits with leverage only after a 2% gain. The manual codifies five tenets: volatility-adjusted unit sizes, mandatory written stops and profit objectives, a minimum 4-to-1 reward/risk ratio, daily re-evaluation of every position from the close, and pre-determined drawdown interventions (forced liquidation, sit-outs, reduced risk parameters at 10/15/20/25% off peak NAV). Later sections cover trading psychology, common psychological barriers (overtrading, fear, ego), self-evaluation questionnaires, the 'Power 5' daily ritual, and position-management worksheets.

  • PTJ's asymmetric plan: risk 2.5% of capital to make 30%; each drawdown breach triggers a time-out, written analysis, and tighter limits.
  • Never fall behind early in the year; press early profits into leveraged bets — the only path to a home-run year.
  • No trade without a written stop and profit objective, and a reward/risk ratio of at least 4-to-1.
  • Size positions by volatility-adjusted units of risk (percent of capital), never by arbitrary contract counts.
  • Re-evaluate every open position daily using the close as a fresh entry; portfolio reward/risk must stay high.
  • Hard drawdown rules: at 10/15/20/25% off peak NAV, liquidate, sit out, cut risk parameters, and write a post-mortem.
  • Good trading begets good trading; forced deleveraging in losing streaks protects traders from their own psychology.
  • Most trader failure is behavioural: blaming markets, overtrading, ego over P&L, and lack of daily discipline.

Michael J. Mauboussin, Legg Mason Capital Management (Mauboussin on Strategy, February 1, 2006)

Size Matters: The Kelly Criterion and the Importance of Money Management

Mauboussin argues that maximizing long-term wealth requires two skills: finding an edge and sizing positions properly when you have one — and that most portfolio managers neglect the second. Using a coin-toss example, he derives the Kelly formula (edge/odds = fraction to bet), showing that betting too little leaves money on the table while overbetting a positive-expectancy game reduces wealth and can cause ruin. He contrasts Kelly's geometric-mean maximization with Markowitz mean/variance analysis, which ignores compounding and assumes normal distributions, and reviews Samuelson's utility-based objections and Thorp's practitioner success. Practical barriers — open-end fund incentives, short-term focus, loss aversion and myopic portfolio checking — explain why few investors apply Kelly. Cites Buffett's quarter-of-assets American Express bet as sizing done right.

  • Wealth building needs two things: an analytical edge, and allocating the right amount of capital when you have one.
  • Kelly formula: bet fraction f = edge/odds; it maximizes long-run geometric growth of capital.
  • Overbetting a positive expected-value opportunity reduces wealth and can guarantee ruin — likely killer of several hedge funds.
  • If you reinvest (parlay) your bets, maximize geometric mean, not arithmetic mean; mean/variance ignores compounding.
  • A single zero payoff makes the geometric mean zero: play long enough and you are ruined.
  • Kelly requires repeated trials, a long horizon, and knowing the distribution; real markets have fat tails.
  • Kelly returns are a roller coaster; loss aversion makes full-Kelly psychologically brutal, so many use partial Kelly.
  • Greater opportunity deserves a larger bet — Buffett put ~25% of assets in American Express in the mid-1960s.

Riaz Ahmad & Paul Wilmott, Wilmott magazine (Technical Article)

Which Free Lunch Would You Like Today, Sir?: Delta Hedging, Volatility Arbitrage and Optimal Portfolios

A classic Wilmott magazine technical paper on how to actually monetize a volatility forecast via delta hedging. When an option is mispriced, the trader must choose a 'hedging volatility': hedging with actual (forecast) volatility locks in a guaranteed total profit equal to the Black-Scholes value difference, but produces wild mark-to-market swings along the way; hedging with implied volatility gives a smooth, always-positive daily P&L, but the final profit is random and path-dependent, driven by where the stock's drift takes gamma. The authors derive closed-form formulas for the expected profit and variance of profit for single options and portfolios, examine hedging with intermediate volatilities, quantify how skew and strike affect risk/reward, and show portfolio optimizations (mean-variance, probability targets, exponential utility) that naturally discover trades like risk reversals.

  • Hedge with actual volatility: final profit is guaranteed, but mark-to-market P&L is random and can look ugly mid-trade.
  • Hedge with implied volatility: daily P&L is smooth and always positive, but total profit is path-dependent and unknown in advance.
  • Hedging with implied vol needs only the direction of your view (actual above or below implied), easing forecasting pressure.
  • Expected profit from implied-vol hedging peaks when drift carries the stock toward the strike, where gamma is largest.
  • Mark-to-market constraints from risk managers or investors usually push practitioners toward implied-volatility hedging.
  • Profit is often insensitive to the exact hedging volatility chosen — there is considerable leeway before you risk losing money.
  • Closed-form expectation and variance formulas enable option-portfolio optimization; one example naturally recovers an ideal risk-reversal trade.
  • Only gamma matters when hedging with implied vol, so calls and puts of the same strike are interchangeable.
